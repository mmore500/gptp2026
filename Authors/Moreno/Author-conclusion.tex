In the first case study incorporating best-effort communication on MPI multiprocessing across traditional CPU-based HPC nodes, we saw that
\begin{enumerate}
\item best-effort communication and synchronization improve scaling efficiency for both communication-heavy workloads and compute-heavy problems with heterogeneous workloads;
\item quality of service metrics scale stably with processor count;
\item in a benchmark problem with an objective solution quality score, the trade-off between quality-of-service and efficiency was such that best-effort approaches were able to produce higher-quality solutions within an allotted runtime;
\item quality of service metrics were able to flag potential biasing artifacts, such as emergent producer-consumer imbalances; and
\item quality of service metrics flagged an anomalous laggard node, and allowed us to determine that quality of service and performance nonetheless remained stable across the computation.
\end{enumerate}
In the second case study incorporating best-effort strategies into the memory- and bandwidth-constrained Cerebras Wafer-Scale Engine, we found that
\begin{enumerate}
\item inferential observability strategies, inspired by techniques biologists use to study the evolutionary history of biological organisms, allow us to overcome limitations in data volume and stochasticity to usefully capture evolutionary history (phylogeny reconstruction);
\item inferential observability, combined with quality-of-service tracking, allow us to detect and correct potential biasing factors arising from asymmetries in directional migration rates; and
\item inferential observability allows us to easily workaround hardware faults causing corruption of data exported from a subset of the Wafer-Scale Engine by simply excluding it.
\end{enumerate}

Although practical scaling challenges explored in this work are, to an extent, universal across HPC problem domains, the suitability of the best-effort model varies greatly from application to application.
Some domains are clear cut in favor of the reliable digital machine model --- for example, due to regulatory issues \cite{dongarra2014applied}.
However, a subset of HPC applications can tolerate --- or even harness --- occasionally flawed or even fundamentally nondeterministic computation \cite{chakradhar2010best}.
Various approximation algorithms or heuristics fall into this category, with notable work being done on best-effort stochastic gradient descent for artificial neural network applications \cite{dean2012large,zhao2019elastic,niu2011hogwild,noel2014dogwild,rhodes2020real}.
Likewise, algorithms relying on pseudo-stochastic methods that tend to exploit noise (rather than destabilize due to it) also make good candidates \cite{chakrapani2008probabilistic,chakradhar2010best}.

Within this group, certain characteristics of digital evolution make it uniquely suited to best-effort computing approaches.
Digital evolution falls into the latter case of systems well-suited to best-effort methods, as evolving systems already require noise to fuel variation.
Complex biological organisms exhibit characteristic best-effort properties: trillions of cells interact asynchronously while overcoming all but the most extreme failures in a noisy world.
As such, bio-inspired algorithms present strong potential to benefit from best-effort communication strategies.
For example, evolutionary algorithms commonly use guided stochastic methods (i.e., selection and mutation operators) resulting in a search process that does not guarantee optimality, but typically produces a diverse range of high-quality results.
Further, among all such methods, contingent and stochastic dynamics, in which small differences can propagate out into profound effects can make meaningful identical outcomes dubious at scale unless huge effort is invested to ensure bit-level reproducibility.

Among disruptions encountered by biological evolution \cite{TODO}, the scale of node outages and network fluctuations pale in comparison.
It is certainly the case that large-scale disruptions can fundamentally redirect and systematically bias evolutionary outcomes.
As such, digital evolution projects will vary in their tolerance for best-effort relaxation.
For simulations meant to directly and literally depict a specific biological analog --- in order to study it --- flexibility will be lower.
However, the particular domains of artificial life and evolutionary computation enjoy much greater epistemological flexibility.
In the case of evolutionary computation, evolution is used as a tool rather than, necessarily, as an object of scientific study.
Therefore, the worst outcome is that disruptions produce a bad-quality outcome --- which can be detected according to objective evaluation criteria and rejected.
Likewise, the best outcome can be selected from among several replicates and so occasional disruptions --- even if extreme --- are not necessarily concerning.
In the case of artificial life, computational simulacrums are taken as an instance of an evolving system, for which noise and disruption are valid conditions, and therefore have greater freedom to be evaluated on their own terms \cite{pennock2007models}.
It is certainly the case that hardware-level disruptions could cause confounding effects in experiments conducted using artificial life systems, but it is not the case that disruptions threaten the overall epistemological validity of the system in the first place, as artificial life simulations commonly rely on a bottom-up approach and seek to model life-as-it-could-be evolving in a noisy environment akin to the natural world, yet distinct from it \cite{bonabeau1994we}.

Indeed, best-effort, real-time computing approaches have also been used in some artificial life models \cite{ray1995proposal,TODODISHTINY}, with particularly notable work in recent years led by Dave Ackley \cite{Ackley2023,Ackley2016}.
Likewise, island model genetic algorithms are easy to parallelize and have been shown to perform well with asynchronous migration \cite{izzo2009parallel}.

Although perfect reproducibility and observability have uniquely enabled digital evolution experiments to ask and answer otherwise intractable questions \cite{pontes2020evolutionary,lenski2003evolutionary,grabowski2013case,dolson2020interpreting,fortuna2019coevolutionary,goldsby2014evolutionary,covert2013experiments,zaman2011rapid,bundy2021footprint,dolson2017spatial}, the reliable digital machine model is not strictly necessary for all such work.
With respect to hypothesis-driven digital evolution work --- whether in evolutionary biology, or in developing evolutionary computation methods --- robust existing methods and statistics for accounting for data limitations in field biology, experimental evolution, and public health make simulations in the sphere of ecology, evolution, and epidemiology an ideal testbed for applications in HPC.
For instance, evolutionary epidemiologists working \textit{in vivo} already employ analogous, mature sampling-based surveillance methods (e.g., wastewater monitoring, viral sequencing) as do ecologists (e.g., mark recapture, quadrats, metagenomics) and evolutionary biologists (e.g., phylodynamics, \textit{ex situ} cultivation, biorepositories).

As has been pointed out by Dave Ackley \cite{TODO}, for these reasons artificial life and, more broadly, digital evolution has a unique opportunity to play in exploring and pushing the boundaries of post-deterministic computing.
Ackley's work \cite{machinaTODO}, as with most existing studies of best-effort computing \cite{chippa2014scalable,ackley2011homeostatic,cho2012ersa,chakrapani2008probabilistic,rhodes2020real}, has focused on bespoke experimental hardware.
By contrast, here, we focus on exploring best-effort relaxations among parallel and distributed elements within existing, commercially-available hardware at the heart of mainstream high-performance computing, including both traditional CPU-based HPC and the emerging next-generation Cerebras Wafer-Scale Engine hardware accelerator platform.
Developing large-scale, high-power hardware requires enormous investment, and so it is not surprising that the most powerful available hardware is designed around mainstream use cases in traditional high-performance computing and, increasingly, deep learning-powered AI/ML use cases.
Insofar as the most ambitious objectives in realizing truly vast computational systems, the most pragmatic way forward is to show how traditional systems can benefit from adopting best-effort relaxations --- and influence further development of these mainstream platforms in that direction --- rather than building from scratch.
While, as we have shown here, some use cases for mainstream HPC hardware can benefit from best-effort relaxations, existing software libraries, though, do not explicitly expose a convenient best-effort communication interface for such work.
Where effort is needed, therefore, is in algorithm design and software development --- and hardware will follow.
By translating these approaches into mainstream high-performance computing writ large, digital evolution may play a role in laying a principled, rigorous, and generalizable foundation for broader development of post-deterministic computing writ large.
