\section{Best-effort Case Study: Accelerator-based HPC} \label{moreno:sec:case2}

\input{fig/wse.tex}

In addition to synchronization costs, as highlighted in our first case study (Section \ref{moreno:sec:case1}), best-effort strategies can also be leveraged to accommodate constraints of memory scarcity and data loss from hardware faults.
These factors have come to the fore in ongoing work conducting agent-based evolution experiments on the Cerebras Wafer-Scale Engine.
This hardware, primarily developed to target AI/ML workloads, exemplifies the emerging promise of massively-distributed compute power, but also lays bare accompanying challenges.

The 3rd generation Cerebras WSE comprises a $755 \times 1{\small,}170$ mesh of 880{\small,}000 networked compute cores (Processing Elements or PEs; Figure \ref{moreno:fig:wse:architecture}).
PEs execute independently with CPU-like control flow, and use specialized on-chip routing to stream 32-bit messages between neighbors with single-cycle latency. % with vectorized integer and floating-point operations available
% In the context of digital evolution, this architecture is a natural fit for island model schemes \cite{bennett1999building}, where each PE hosts an independent subpopulation.
The chip shards on-device memory, 44GB in aggregate, uniformly across PEs --- each equipped with a private memory bank with single-cycle read/write access \cite{buitrago2021neocortex}.
Thus, work on WSE must contend with scarce memory per PE (48kB each), extreme decentralization (direct communication only among local neighbors), and restricted host-device connectivity (only at grid perimeter).
Physical stresses placed on hardware by kilowatts of thermal loading \cite{la2020cerebras}, coupled with sheer component count of transistors, also make hardware faults a practical concern.

Our second case study explores best-effort strategies in overcoming these challenges to collect data from digital evolution experiments conducted on Wafer-Scale Engine hardware.
Experiments draw unreported details from two projects:
\begin{enumerate}
\item work with a dummy agent model, as a vehicle to test methodology for best-effort tracking of evolutionary history \cite{singhvi2025scalable}, and
\item experiments scaling up an existing agent-based model of mutator allele dynamics to explore outcomes in large, spatially-distributed populations \cite{raynes2018sign}.
\end{enumerate}
Both of these applications target the Wafer-Scale Engine using an island model scheme \cite{bennett1999building}, where each PE hosts an independent subpopulation and randomly-selected population members are migrated between neighboring PEs.
Detail on experiment configurations underlying these applications accompanies in supplementary material \cite{moreno_2026_trust}.

Case study material is organized in four parts.
\begin{enumerate}
\item description of approach to characterize evolutionary history based on population samples (Section \ref{moreno:sec:case2:hstrat}),
\item resilience of this approach to sparse, asynchronous device-to-host subsamples of population composition and to a persistent localized data-corruption fault arising across runs in a hardware testbed (Section \ref{moreno:sec:case2:missing}),
\item use of evolutionary tracking to complement direct QoS tracking to identify emergent biases in population dynamics (Section \ref{moreno:sec:case2:bias}), and
\item details of a generalized ring buffer approach used to efficiently record time series data of \textit{a priori} unknown duration by accepting best-effort granularization of data in the case that duration exceeds available capacity (Section \ref{moreno:sec:case2:timeseries}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reconstruction-based Estimation of Evolutionary History}
\label{moreno:sec:case2:hstrat}

% The structure of evolutionary ancestry relationships arising in phylogenies is a cornerstone of inquiry in modern evolutionary biology .
% Phylogenetic analysis is integral to much of evolution research, whether conducted \textit{in vivo} or \textit{in silico} \cite{faithConservationEvaluationPhylogenetic1992, STAMATAKIS2005phylogenetics,frenchHostPhylogenyShapes2023,kim2006discovery,lewinsohnStatedependentEvolutionaryModels2023a,lenski2003evolutionary}.
In our WSE-based experiments, migrations stitching independent subpopulations into a single massive meta-population introduce substantial logistical challenges of tracking evolutionary history as it unfolds.
In this setting, it is particularly important for instrumentation to be lightweight --- resources used for instrumentation (e.g., memory, bandwidth) directly compete with memory used to increase population size and bandwidth that is useful for facilitating on-device migrations.
Even were sufficient memory and bandwidth available, the entirety of evolutionary history --- upwards of a quadrillion replication events in some simulations \cite{singhvi2025scalable} --- would be of negligible tractability in its entirety.
Instead, means are needed to extract a reasonable subsample or summary of evolutionary history.

In evolutionary studies, a key aspect of history is phylogeny --- the structure of ancestry relationships among organisms \cite{faithConservationEvaluationPhylogenetic1992, STAMATAKIS2005phylogenetics,frenchHostPhylogenyShapes2023,kim2006discovery,lenski2003evolutionary}.
In addition to tracing the history of evolutionary events \cite{dawkins2016ancestor,hedges2015tree,hinchliff2015synthesis}, phylogenetic analysis can characterize more general questions about the underlying mode and tempo of evolution \cite{felsenstein1985phylogenies}.
Classically, these analyses have been applied to investigation of species-level macroevolutionary dynamics revolving around speciation and extinction rates; however, population- and organism-level dynamics can also be inferred, such as the spread of beneficial mutations within a population or fitness parameters like growth rate and probability of survival \cite{genthon2023cell, levy2015quantitative,stadler2013recovering,Moreno2025ecology,lewinsohnStatedependentEvolutionaryModels2023a,nozoe2017inferring}.
Phylogeny data has also been leveraged in proactive efforts to influence the trajectory of evolving populations \cite{Scott2018}.
% Such scenarios arise in clinical and public health fields, managing therapy-resistant tumors, antibiotic resistance, and infectious disease \cite{Scott2018}.
% Notable objective-oriented contexts arise also in computing, where evolution can be leveraged as an optimization algorithm.
In application-oriented evolutionary computation, phylogenetic information can be used directly in guiding evolution toward desired outcomes \cite{lalejini2024phylogeny,lalejini2024runtime,murphy2008simple,burke2003increased}, but also indirectly to provide diagnostics for choosing appropriate configuration and parameterizations \cite{hernandez2022can,shahbandegan2022untangling}.
Digital simulations also serve as a useful testbed for bioinformatics methodologies, as ground truth conditions may be configured directly \cite{daudey2024aevol,haller2023slim,moreno2025extending}.

% Our ultimate goal of building these ancestry trees is to provide visibility into large-scale evolution experiments running on the WSE.
% Structural composition of ancestry trees can reveal key evolutionary dynamics, such as discovery of beneficial mutations (adaptation).
% Notably, in this domain, phylogeny data underpins core metrics for measuring ongoing system change \cite{dolson2019modes}.
% In the bioinformatics testbed domain, which also makes heavy use of phylogeny data, limitations of simulation scale can obscure the relationships of population size and sampling density with sensitivity and accuracy \cite{moreno2025extending,TODO}.

To collect ancestry data from WSE experiments, we have adopted strategies inspired by real-world experiments that assume data collection is expensive, limited, and non-deterministic.
Rather than exhaustively tracking parent-child relationships \cite{dolson2023phylotrackpy}, we adopt an approach akin to how biologists reconstruct ancestry by comparing DNA sequences --- which we call ``hereditary stratigraphy.''
Inspired by synthetic biology techniques for lineage tracking \cite{Masuyama2019}, we use genome barcoding with single-bit markers to shrink memory footprint as low as 96 bits per organism \cite{moreno2025testing}; once budgeted storage capacity fills, barcodes are progressively pruned to maintain an approximate record \cite{moreno2024algorithms}.
Barcodes remain chronologically attributed, allowing estimation of the point in time when lineages diverged from conflicts \cite{moreno2022hereditary}.
Reconstruction of phylogenies comprising many taxa can then be accomplished through an agglomerative trie-based algorithm \cite{moreno2023toward}.

\subsection{Robustness of Best-effort Estimation of Evolutionary History}
\label{moreno:sec:case2:missing}

A key advantage of reconstruction-based evolutionary analysis is flexibility with respect to the number of taxa (phylogenetic units) sampled.
At the extremum, evolutionary divergence can be estimated between a single pair of taxa.
Ultimately, necessary data collection arises from experimental objectives --- for instance, taxa may be sampled through time, or only the final time point.

Because it tolerates sparse, irregular sampling, reconstruction-based analysis is well suited to best-effort data collection strategies.
% on large WSE-generated datasets
For experiments benchmarking barcode-based phylogeny reconstruction \cite{singhvi2025scalable}, sample genomes were gathered from WSE-based simulation on a rolling basis using asynchronous device-to-host (D2H) transfers (Figure \ref{moreno:fig:wse:phylogeny}).
This approach allowed available bandwidth capacity to be dynamically balanced with existing on-chip traffic.
Experiments evaluated a population size of 190.8 million for 5 million generations, elapsing around 8{\small,}750 generations per second walltime.
D2H transfers sampled one genome per PE, and completed on the order of fifteen times per second --- yielding 9{\small,}000 snapshots over the course of 10 minute runtime.
This volume of harvested genomes, nearly 8 billion in total, exceeded downstream capacity, and so was thinned on a rolling basis to 1 billion genomes (~0.0001\% of cumulative individuals).

Another practical benefit of reconstruction-based analysis is resilience to data loss from hardware faults.
Reported phylogeny experiments were conducted using system \texttt{rwse1001-cs-sy01} hosted in Cerebras Cloud.
Between February 20th and 26th, 2026 this hardware began to produce unreliable D2H transfers localized along row 410 between columns 0 and 248 (Figure \ref{moreno:fig:wse:fault}).
Between 5 and 6\% of 32-bit values copied from this region encountered bit-flip errors, corrupting around 0.03\% of overall extracted genomes.
Runtime parity checks confirmed that this phenomenon did not compromise integrity of on-device simulation, only capability to collect complete data from the chip.

In this case, best-effort resilience allowed work to continue uninterrupted during the following two months the chip remained in service.
While narrow localization simplified handling of this particular fault, a minimally invasive checksum strategy could be used to handle more generalized transfer faults with low runtime cost:
each PE would maintain a running checksum over its exported data, and faults could be detected (at coarse granularity, barring rare collisions) by collecting and auditing final checksum values.

Hardware faults are an inescapable consideration in modern high-performance computing \cite{gupta2017failures,navaux2023challenges}, with upwards of daily occurrence in large systems \cite{kokolis2025revisiting}.
Faults are typically handled by checkpoint rollbacks \cite{gupta2017failures,zhu2025understanding} --- in flagship workloads, consuming on the order of 100PB of high-bandwidth disk capacity \cite{frontiere2025cosmological}.
Further, in highly integrated hardware accelerator platforms, such as the WSE, challenges are compounded by the fact that small component failures can compromise much larger hardware units \cite{cerebras2025yield}.
Where computation can be structured to gracefully degrade under disruption, such costs can be avoided.
Fault robustness may also enable future compatibility with emerging computational substrates that operate at limited fidelity \cite{markovi2020physics,ackley2011pursue,dreslinski2010near}.

\subsection{Detection of Bias in Population Dynamics}
\label{moreno:sec:case2:bias}

In addition to benchmarking the speed of barcode-based reconstruction on large wafer-scale-generated datasets, we wished to perform further work to validate the plausibility of reconstructed phylogenies.
While earlier CPU-based work has extensively validated underlying methodology against ground truth comparisons \cite{moreno2025testing}, at scale on the WSE ground truth data was not available.
So, instead, we ran simulations under different evolutionary conditions and looked more generally at the structure of the phylogeny.
Under conditions of local migration, one pattern that we would expect to see in a phylogeny is spatial proximity among related taxa \cite{novembre2008genes,grundler2025geographic}.

We began by examining evolutionary histories under neutral conditions, where no advantageous mutations were available.
Under these conditions, lineage histories constitute a random walk in space \cite{lemey2010phylogeography}.
Color-coding \cite{steiger2015explorative} by the position of samples on the WSE revealed a surprising pattern, where parallel lineages appeared to disproportionately transition through common gradients (left panels, Figure \ref{moreno:fig:wse:bias}).
Closer inspection of sample lineages revealed that many progressed physically across the chip from bottom-to-top (right panels, Figure \ref{moreno:fig:wse:bias}), consistent with a scenario where migration in this direction was slightly more likely.

This finding demonstrates the importance of diagnostic checks on the structure of underlying computation.
% Such bias can be easily prevented by throttling each PEs outgoing migration rates to their slowest direction, which we have enacted for future simulations.
% Having identified this bias, and noted that it could have a mild, but detectable systematic large-scale effect on evolution, we were able to take action to ameliorate this.
% In this case, symmetry in directional migration rates could be rectified in a simple manner --- without invoking global synchronization --- by simply restricting the send rate of each PE to its neighbor to the slowest among neighbors.
While Quality of Service metrics can flag this kind of issue, their interpretation requires testing against particular hypotheses for biases.
A key direction for future work should be curation of a standardized panel of QoS diagnostic tests --- such as incoming/outgoing traffic imbalance --- that provide a general-purpose starting point to characterize QoS dynamics and flag potential asymmetries and anomalies.
However, as illustrated in this example, instrumentation-level analyses in best-effort systems should be coupled with skeptical inspection of simulation dynamics to identify potential biasing factors in a best-effort computation to guard against unknown unknowns.

A key challenge in this work was balancing limited on-device memory between simulation content and data recording.
For example, some experiments required time series measurements of mutator prevalence in PE subpopulations.
To economize memory use, we applied algorithms that generalize ring buffer storage to efficiently perform systematic temporal downsampling \cite{moreno2024algorithms,gunther2014algorithm}.
Successive local samples are recorded as single bits within a flat, fixed-size buffer --- with no bookkeeping overhead.
Crucially, because recording duration (i.e., until mutator allele extinction) is unknown \textit{a priori}, this approach dynamically coarsens recording density via overwrites once storage capacity is reached --- strictly bounding memory use.

Among other results, this data collection strategy revealed that even in circumstances where mutator alleles did not reliably fix, they could nonetheless transiently dominate population composition --- in some cases, reaching peaks upwards of 99.9\% frequency (Figure \ref{moreno:fig:wse:timeseries}).
