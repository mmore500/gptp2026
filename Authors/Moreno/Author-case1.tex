\section{Case Study: Cluster-based HPC} \label{moreno:sec:case1}

\input{fig/cpu.tex}

Evolutionary transitions in individuality, such as multicellularity and eusociality, are broadly considered as a key facet of the surprising constructive power of biological evolution \cite{taylor2016open}.
Digital evolution experiments offer a uniquely tractable opportunity to examine how these events can unfold.
Such experiments, however, can become computationally intense: life history of each multicell encompasses an independent subpopulation of replicators --- possibly themselves carrying out hegerogenous, interaction-intensive developmental, physiological, and ecological processes.
Infrastructure for such experiments therefore provides a germane testbed  to explore HPC methods for digital evolution.

As a first case study, we test the performance characteristics of a best-effort multiprocessing framework developed to support the DISHTINY project (DIStributed Heirarchical Transitions in INdividualitY) \cite{moreno2019toward}.
This system tracks a fixed-capacity population of digital cells distributed across a toroidal grid, which can sense attributes of their immediate neighbors, communicate with those neighbors through message passing, and interact with neighboring cells cooperatively or competitively \cite{moreno2021exploring}.
Under the hood, event-driven linear genetic programs control cell behavior \cite{lalejini2018evolving}.

To support multiprocessing, DISHTINY routes all cell-cell interaction through a communication layer implemented by the Conduit library \cite{moreno2021conduit}.
\unskip\footnote{%
This library is one among many frameworks have arisen to offer useful parallel and distributed programming abstractions, including task-based frameworks \cite{kale1993charm++,bauer2012legion,blumofe1996cilk,reinders2007intel} or domain-specific programming languages \cite{el2006upc,chamberlain2007parallel}.
However, these frameworks assume a deterministic relationship between tasks or otherwise forbid data races.
}
This multiprocessing framework exposes a best-effort interface backed by Message Passing Interface (MPI) intrinsics \cite{gropp1996high}.
In the case where two neighboring toroidal grid cells reside on different executing processes, a configurable-capacity send buffer asynchronously dispatches requested cell-cell interactions and updates to local state.
In the case where send buffer capacity is full, communication may be configured to drop rather than blocking.
\unskip\footnote{%
MPI guarantees delivery of dispatched messages --- network-layer best-effort relaxations would involve lower-level protocols like InfiniBand Unreliable Datagrams \cite{kashyap2006ip,koop2007high}.
}

Conduit-based profiling experiments investigated four questions (1) can best-effort relazations improve scaling efficiency, (2) can quality of service metrics meaningfully detect runtime irregularities introduced by best-effort relaxation, (3) does median quality of service scale robustly, and (4) is median quality of service robust to individual hardware anamolies at scale?

\subsection{Profiling Experiment Design}

In this case study, we use DISHTINY simulations as a vehicle to investigate the performance characteristics of best-effort communication.
For profiling trials, randomly-initialized populations were evaluated under default conditions over a five-minute execution window.
Conduit communication was instrumented with counters to track elapsed send, receive, and round-trip touch
\unskip\footnote{%
To avoid clock synchronization issues, no cross-process durations timings were taken.
Instead, runtime instrumentation maintained independent zero-initialized ``touch counters,'' appended to all dispatched messages.
By incrementing this value upon receipt, it increases by two for each successful round trip completed.
}
counts.
In addition to recording final counter values at completion, once per minute a background thread polled counter values bookending a one-second ``snapshot'' interval.

To reflect workloads typical of island-model evolutionary computation \cite{bennett1999building}, DISHTINY profiling assigned a grid size of 3{\small,}600 cells per process (a widely-used default \cite{ofria2004avida}).
To efficiency, Conduit was configured to consolidate communication traffic between neighboring processes to bulk transfers once per update cycle.
This benchmark thus reflects a compute-intensive workload.

To survey communication-intensive workloads, we supplemented DISHTINY trials with a lightweight graph coloring algorithm designed to deconflict WLAN networks \cite{leith2012wlan}.
Nodes, also arranged in a toroidal lattice, begin with one of three possible randomly-assigned colors.
In the case of conflict between neighbors, nodes probabilistically self-reassign a new color.
As another benefit, this benchmark provides a succinct measure of solution quality: the number of remaining same-color neighbors.

Futher details on benchmark methodology are included with Supplemental Material \cite{moreno_2026_trust}.

\subsection{Calculating Proposed Metrics}

In this section, we outline quality-of-service metrics considered in our analysis (Figure \ref{moreno:fig:cpu:best-effort-qos}).
A more complete description is provided in Supplemental Material \cite{moreno_2026_trust}.
For the purposes of these metrics, we assume that simulations proceed in an iterative fashion with alternating compute and communication cycles.
Metrics are collected across a ``snapshot'' window --- here, we focus on aggregations over runtime; however, brief point-in-time snapshots are also possible.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Throughput}: \textit{update time}, amount of wall-time elapsed per simulation compute/communication cycle averaged across a snapshot window ();
\item \textbf{Latency}: \textit{update-latency} or \textit{walltime-latency}, duration elapsed between message dispatch and message delivery, calculated from a round-trip measure to avoid clock synchronization interference;
\item \textbf{Attrition}: \textit{drop fraction}, proportion of messages sent that are dropped (e.g., when a send buffer fills); and
\item \textbf{Bunching}: \textit{consolidation}, the proportion of possible independent delivery events unrealized due to batching.
\end{enumerate}

First, we sought to determine whether best-effort communication strategies can benefit performance compared to the traditional perfect communication model.
We considered two measures of performance: computational steps executed per unit time and, for the graph-coloring problem, solution quality achieved within a fixed-duration run window.
We focus on weak scaling, growing overall problem size proportional to processor count.
(Put another way, we hold problem size per processor constant.)
% \unskip\footnote{
% As opposed to strong scaling, where the problem size is held fixed while processor count increases.
% }
This approach prevents interference from shifts in processes' workload profiles in observation of the effects of scaling up processor count.
We focus on multiprocessing, and to test under the most difficult conditions we assign each process to a distinct node.

Second, we sought to more closely characterize variability in message dispatch, transmission, and delivery under the best-effort model using the quality-of-service metrics proposed in Section \ref{moreno:sec:introduction}.
In particular, we extend our understanding of performance scaling from the preceding experiments by analyzing how each quality of service metric fares as problem size and processor count grow together, a ``weak scaling'' experiment.
This analysis would detect a scenario where raw performance remains stable under weak scaling, but quality of service (and, therefore, potentially quality of computation) degrades.

\subsection{Scaling Efficiency and Solution Quality}

Best-effort communication and synchronization improve scaling efficiency for both communication-heavy workloads and compute-heavy problems with heterogenous workloads.
In a benchmark problem with an objective solution quality score, the trade-off between quality-of-service and efficiency was such that best-effort approaches were able to produce higher-quality solutions within an alloted runtime.
We tested how performance on the graph coloring and digital evolution benchmarks fared when scaling with fully independent processes located on different hardware nodes.

Figure \ref{moreno:fig:cpu:TODO} shows per-CPU algorithm update rate for the graph coloring benchmark at 1, 4, 16, and 64 processes.
Multiprocess graph coloring exhibits consistent update-rate performance across process counts under the independent-execution control, where inter-CPU communication is entirely disabled.
This matches the unsurprising expectation that, indeed, with comparable hardware a single process should exhibit the same mean performance as any number of completely decoupled processes.
At 64 processes, with best-effort asynchronicity the graph coloring benchmark exhibits about 63\% the update-rate performance of single-process execution.
This represents a $7.8\times$ speedup compared to full-synchronicity, significantly better at 4, 16, and 64 processes ($p < 0.05$, bootstrap test).
Likewise, shown in Figure \ref{moreno:fig:cpu:TODO}, best-effort asynchronicity yields significantly better graph-coloring results within the allotted time at 4, 16, and 64 processes ($p < 0.05$, bootstrap test).

Figure \ref{moreno:fig:cpu:TODO} presents per-CPU algorithm update rate for the digital evolution benchmark at 1, 4, 16, and 64 processes.
Relative performance fares well at high process counts under this relatively computation-heavy workload.
With 64 processes, fully best-effort simulation retains about 92\% the update rate performance of single-process simulation.
This represents a $2.1\times$ speedup compared to the fully-synchronous run mode 0.
Best-effort mode significantly outperforms fully-synchronous mode at both 16 and 64 processes ($p < 0.05$, bootstrap test).

\subsection{Quality-of-Service Scaling}

Multiprocess performance scales well under the best-effort approach, with overlapping performance estimate intervals for 16 and 64 processor counts on both surveyed benchmark problems.
This section aims to flesh out a more holistic picture of the effects of increasing processor count on best-effort computation by considering a comprehensive suite of quality of service metrics.
Our particular interest is in which, if any, aspects of quality of service degrade under larger processing pools.

To address these questions, we performed weak scaling experiments on 16, 64, and 256 processes using the graph coloring benchmark.

\subsubsection{Update Time}

Figure \ref{moreno:fig:cpu:TODO} shows the distribution of walltime update rates observed --- across process counts, around 200 microseconds.
We performed an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression to test how mean update time changed with processor count.
In all cases, update time increased significantly with processor count from 16 to 64 to 256 CPUs.
Between 64 and 256 processes, however, update time instead decreased.
Median update time exhibited the same relationships with processor count, tested with quartile regression.

\subsubsection{Update-Latency}

For the graph coloring benchmark, update-latency sits around 1.2 updates.
However, at 256 processes, outliers arose up to almost 8{\small,}000 update simstep latency.
Over 16, 64, and 256 processes, OLS regression indicates mean simstep latency increased with process count.
However, median latency did not increase with process count, tested via quartile regression.

\subsubsection{Time-Latency}

Walltime latency sits at 2ms.
At 256 processes, outliers of more than 10s occur.

We performed OLS regressions to test how mean time-latency changed with processor count.
Over 16, 64, and 256 processes, mean time-latency increased significantly with processor count only with 2{\small,}048 simels per CPU.
By contrast, median time-latency was stable across processor counts (quartile regression).
Over 16, 64, and 256 processes, median walltime latency increased significantly only with 4 CPUs per node and 2{\small,}048 simels per CPU.
Over 64 and 256 processes, there was no significant relationship between processor count and median walltime latency under any condition.

\subsubsection{Drop Fraction}

Typical delivery failure rate was near zero.
However, outlier delivery failure rates of up to 0.7 were observed at 256 processes.
% Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:weak-scaling-distribution-delivery-failure-rate} shows the distribution of delivery failure rates observed across run conditions.
Mean delivery failure rate increased significantly between 64 and 256 processes.
However, the median delivery failure rate remained stable across processor counts.

\subsubsection{Delivery Bunching}

Median delivery bunchiness was around 0.4, meaning TODO.
Using OLS regression, we found no evidence of mean bunching worsening with increased process count.
In fact, over 16, 64, and 256 processes bunchiness significantly decreased with process count (both mean and median regressions).

\subsection{Quality of Service: Faulty Hardware}
\label{moreno:sec:with-lac-417-vs-sans-lac-417}

The extreme magnitude of outliers observed in 256-process allocations prompted further investigation of the conditions under which these outliers arose.
Closer inspection revealed that the most extreme outliers were all associated with snapshots on a single node: lac-417.
So, we performed further tests acquiring two separate 256 process allocations on the lac cluster: one including lac-417 and one excluding lac-417.

Figure \ref{moreno:fig:cpu:TODO} compare the distributions of quality of service metrics between allocations with and without lac-417.
Extreme outliers are present exclusively in the lac-417 allocation for walltime latency, simstep latency, and delivery failure rate.
Otherwise, the metrics' distributions across snapshots are very similar between allocations.
Mean update-latency, time-latency, and delivery failure rate are all significantly greater with lac-417.
Surprisingly, though, update time is significantly shorter with lac-417.

However, there is no significant difference in median value for any quality of service metric between allocations including or excluding lac-417.
This stability of metric medians within allocations containing lac-417 --- which have significantly different means due to outlier values induced by the presence of lac-417 --- demonstrates how the best-effort system maintains overall quality of service stability despite defective or degraded components.

\subsection{Produer-Consumer Imbalance}

In addition to anamolous hardware, quality of service metrics can flag potential more widespread asymmetries emerging in best-effort systems.
For instance, under communication-heavy TODO conditions, we observed a significant negative correlation between incoming and outgoing message rates (Figure \ref{moreno:fig:cpu:TODO}).
This pattern could indicate emergent producer-consumerimbalances between processors, where certain processors become bogged down processing the incoming message payload from other processes that they are able to send fewer messages, allowing the partner process to continue sending messages at a high rate --- a self-reinforcing imbalance that can arise when processing incoming messages has comparable or greater cost than sending them.
Such correlation, however, did not arise under TODO conditions.
